Let's publish some files. Say you have a slide deck, a photo, and a short text note, and you want to hand out links to them. There are four steps: sign in, add funds, upload, and share. The first two you only do once.

<break time="1.5s" />

Hash Bin doesn't have its own passwords. You sign in with an account you already have, such as Google. That gives you a dashboard, and the parts we'll use today are Add Funds and My Uploads.

Remember, you only need an account to publish. The people you share links with won't need one.

<break time="1.5s" />

Publishing is paid from a prepaid balance, so next, add some funds. The minimum is one dollar. The payment goes through Stripe, and the card processing fee is added on top and shown before you pay: two point nine percent plus thirty cents.

That fixed thirty cents is why small deposits look expensive. A one-dollar credit costs a dollar thirty-three. A ten-dollar credit costs ten fifty-nine. Either way, your balance gets the full credit amount.

Payments are final, and a balance can't be withdrawn. But it doesn't expire, either. It just sits there until you publish something. And as we'll see, a dollar of storage goes a long way.

<break time="1.5s" />

Now the upload page. You can drop in several files at once, up to ninety megabytes each.

The first thing that happens is that your browser works out each file's two-fifty-six-tee string, on your own computer, before the file itself is sent. That's how the page knows a few things right away. The text note is so small that it fits inside its own link, so it's free. If you add the same photo twice, the page notices they're identical and uploads it once.

Next, choose how long to keep the files. Retention is bought in whole months, and one period applies to every file in this upload. Pick a month, a year, five years, ten. The total updates as you go.

And if one of these files was already on Hash Bin, maybe because someone else uploaded it, you don't store a second copy. Uploading it adds your chosen months to the existing copy's expiration date instead, at the same price.

<break time="1.5s" />

Click upload, and the cost comes out of your balance: for a few ordinary files kept for a year, a few cents.

Each file gets a content page. It shows the two-fifty-six-tee string, which Hash Bin also calls the content ID, with a button to copy it. You'll also see the file's size, when it expires, and a download link. Everything you've uploaded is listed under My Uploads, so you don't need to keep track of the strings yourself.

<break time="1.5s" />

To share a file, share its link. There are two kinds. The two-fifty-six-tee dot U S link is the file itself. Add an extension to the end, like dot P D F, and browsers will know what kind of file to expect. The hash bin dot org info link is the content's page, where people can see how long it will be kept, and help keep it around.

Whoever you send it to just opens it. No account, no sign-in, no fee.
